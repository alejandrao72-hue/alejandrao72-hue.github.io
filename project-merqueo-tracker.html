The Problem

Users didn't know
where their order was

Merqueo's order tracking screen showed four states — Received, Picked, Dispatched, Delivered — but gave users almost no information about what was actually happening at each step. The result: 16% of all support tickets were people asking "where is my order?" or "when does it get charged?"

Payment confusion was especially severe. Users didn't know when their card would be charged, and seeing unexpected charges triggered panic and support calls — a friction point that impacted both trust and operational costs.

😰 User pain

No clarity on order status

The screen showed 4 statuses but gave no context about what was happening, when, or what to expect next.

💳 User pain

Payment timing was a mystery

Users didn't know when their card would be charged — many assumed it was immediate, which caused confusion and disputes.

📦 User pain

Missing items went unexplained

When products were out of stock and removed from an order, users had no visibility — and found out at delivery or on their bank statement.

📞 Business impact

16% of support load

Status and payment questions were the top driver of customer service contacts — a direct cost that better communication could reduce.

Research Process

From tickets to
real insights

Before designing anything, I dove into the data. I read 20 Zendesk tickets to understand exactly what users were asking, ran user interviews using a mental model exercise ("how do you imagine the process from order to delivery?"), and benchmarked how MeLi, Uber Eats, Rappi, and Domicilios handled the same problem.

01

Zendesk ticket analysis

Read and categorized 20 support tickets to identify the most common pain points. Payment confusion and order status uncertainty emerged as the top two issues — with users often not knowing when the charge would happen or whether their order was being processed.

QuantitativeSupport data
02

Mental model interviews

Asked users to map out how they imagined the order process worked — from purchase to delivery. Responses revealed that users had wildly different mental models, and that their anxiety peaked between placement and payment confirmation.

03

Competitive benchmark

Analyzed order tracking in MeLi, Uber Eats, Rappi, and Domicilios — focusing on how each app communicated status, payment timing, and delivery ETA. Uber Eats' real-time map and Rappi's step-by-step progress bar were key references.

5-second copy test

Once the first scope (copy improvements) was designed, I ran a 5-second test with 17 users to validate whether the new copy was understood — specifically around payment timing and order state descriptions. The test confirmed copy clarity but revealed delivery time information was being missed.

Scope 1 · Copy redesign

Clearer words,
less anxiety

The first scope focused on fixing the copy — adding context and explanation to each order state. Payment timing was surfaced proactively ("we charge your card once your order is picked"), missing items were communicated inline, and cancellation reasons were made explicit for the first time.

Validated with a 5-second test across 17 users — confirming the new copy was understood, and revealing that delivery time information was landing better at the bottom of the screen than in the tracker itself.

Scope 2 · Visual redesign

A tracker that
tells the full story

The second scope was a complete visual overhaul. The vertical step list was replaced with a horizontal progress tracker with 7 distinct states — each accompanied by a contextual illustration that represented what was happening at that moment.

A prominent ETA header was added at the top, a real-time map surfaced when the delivery was en route, and inline alerts proactively communicated missing items, payment status, and anomalies — before users had to call to find out.

Design System · 7 States

Every state,
designed with intent

Defined the information architecture for all 7 order states — each with its own illustration, title, description, and optional contextual alerts. The component system was designed so any combination of payment method and order type could be handled consistently.
